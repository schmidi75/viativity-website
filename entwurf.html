Travel Tips for Motion Sickness

Travel smart. Arrive relaxed. Your well-founded guide for the car, plane, ship, and train.

Know the feeling? As soon as the car starts moving or the ship leaves the port, your stomach gets queasy. You are not alone! Motion sickness – or kinetosis – can cloud the anticipation of any trip, whether you are in a car, train, plane, on a ship, or in a cable car.

Often, it is a simple misunderstanding in your body: Your eyes, for example, see a standstill inside the car, but your inner ear's balance organ feels every curve and movement. Your brain can't properly process these conflicting signals at first and reacts with dizziness or discomfort.

The good news: You don't have to just put up with it. Even small, targeted changes – like the right seat, a steady gaze at the horizon, or a calming breathing technique – can enormously help your body resolve this conflict so you can enjoy your journey again.

General tips for every journey

Being well-prepared makes traveling most relaxing. Many tricks work wonderfully regardless of the mode of transportation and often determine whether a trip is pleasant.

The right snack before the trip

A completely empty stomach often reacts just as sensitively to movement as an overfull one. It is best to treat yourself to a small, easily digestible meal about one to two hours before departure. Rusk, rice, bananas, or light bread are ideal. You should rather avoid very fatty or highly spiced foods beforehand.

Drink enough water

Did you know that even mild dehydration can increase dizziness, fatigue, and discomfort? Therefore, drink small amounts of water regularly before and during the trip. Your body needs this support especially in the heat, on long car rides, or on dry flights.

Start well-rested

A tired mind is more easily unsettled. Sleep deprivation increases the strain on your nervous system and makes you more susceptible to motion sickness. If you travel well-rested, your brain processes movement stimuli much more efficiently and you remain calmer.

The saving gaze into the distance

This is one of the most effective tricks of all: A steady gaze at the horizon or far ahead gives your brain a stable orientation point. This finally allows it to properly classify the perceived movement, and the conflict between your eyes and your balance organ resolves gently.

Did you know?

Motion sickness doesn't just affect children! Around a third of all people develop symptoms under certain conditions – for example, in heavy seas or intense turbulence. Almost anyone can get motion sickness if the movement stimuli are strong enough.

Natural helpers: Ginger and P6 acupressure

Ginger is one of the best-studied natural remedies. Several studies show that ginger can reduce queasiness for many people – whether as a tea, capsule, or candy. Another gentle method is stimulating the P6 point on the wrist, which sends calming signals to the stomach. You can find a simple visual guide on exactly where this point is and how to massage it correctly right in the corresponding tool of the Viativity app.

Cool, fresh air

There is hardly anything more soothing than fresh air when you feel queasy. Cool air immediately improves subjective wellbeing and helps to gently relieve rising feelings of heat.

In the car

Especially as a passenger, you often feel a queasy sensation in your stomach. Your balance organ notices every little curve, while your eyes are frequently focused on the quiet interior of the car. Here is how you can help your body:

Ideally, take the wheel yourself

Those who drive themselves feel unwell much less frequently. As the driver, you observe the road and know seconds in advance when you will brake or steer. Your brain isn't surprised, and your senses work together in harmony.

The passenger seat is your best friend

If someone else is driving, it's best to grab the passenger seat. From there, you have an unobstructed view of the road and the horizon. This provides your brain with exactly the movement information it needs.

Keep your eyes on the road, not your phone

Reading messages or watching videos on a smartphone is unfortunately one of the most common triggers for discomfort in the car. The screen in your hand remains seemingly still while your body moves. You are better off leaning back and enjoying music or an exciting audiobook.

What do recuperation and one-pedal driving have to do with it?

If you quickly feel unwell while driving, the settings of your electric car can play a role. Strong recuperation or one-pedal driving causes the vehicle to brake significantly and automatically when you release the accelerator. These frequent decelerations can cause increased pitching and unfamiliar movements, which can favor motion sickness.

Tip: Choose a milder recuperation level and drive as smoothly as possible. Gentle acceleration and braking make the ride more pleasant and help you arrive relaxed.

On the plane

Even on an airplane, a queasy feeling can occasionally set in. Turbulence, rapid pressure changes, or simply unfamiliar movements can trigger symptoms in sensitive people. How to make your flight more comfortable:

The ideal seat: Over the wings

A seat at the level of the wings is one of the quietest areas in the entire aircraft. Because the plane's center of gravity is located here, you notice turbulence and movements significantly less than, for example, far back in the tail.

Let your gaze wander

If the flight gets bumpy, looking out the window can work wonders. Focus your eyes on the horizon or the cloud cover. This visual confirmation of the flight's movement helps your brain enormously to relax the situation.

Pressure equalization and noise protection

During takeoff and especially during landing, don't forget to swallow or chew regularly to relieve the pressure in your ears. Another insider tip is modern noise-canceling headphones: Less cabin noise means significantly less stress for your head, so you arrive much more relaxed.

On the ship

Seasickness almost always strikes when your balance organ feels the swell, but your eyes only see the rigid walls of a windowless cabin or an interior space. Here are the best tips for the high seas:

The middle of the ship is your friend

Ship movements are not equally strong everywhere. It's best to choose a place in the middle of the ship and on the lower decks, as the rolling and pitching movements are minimal there. You should rather avoid the bow and stern in heavy seas, as it rocks the most there.

Get out into the fresh air

As soon as a slight malaise announces itself, the deck is the best place for you. Take a deep breath. The unobstructed view of the horizon immediately provides your brain with the logical and urgently needed explanation for the rocking.

Good to know: Green apples

Many experienced cruise guests swear by green apples. The pectin and natural fruit acids they contain are often perceived as very pleasant for the stomach. They are even specifically provided on many ships in rough seas.

On the train

A train journey is usually wonderfully relaxing. But especially when we start busying ourselves with other things or ride backwards, our stomach can quickly speak up.

Always sit facing the direction of travel

Our brain naturally expects forward movements. If you ride backwards, it contradicts this expectation and forces your system to reverse signals. Therefore, it is best to always book your seat facing the direction of travel, which makes the ride much more pleasant.

Leave your laptop and smartphone in your bag

Avoid reading, working on your laptop, or intensive use of your smartphone during the journey if possible. Your gaze focuses rigidly on one point while the train rattles and vibrates through curves. This means pure hard work for your balance system.

Keep your head free

Even if it seems wonderfully cozy: You'd better not lean your head directly against the window pane. Otherwise, the fine micro-vibrations of the train are transmitted directly to your skull and additionally irritate the sensitive balance organ in your inner ear.

In the cable car

Rapid changes in altitude, slight swaying, and looking straight down into the depths are a very unique challenge for our senses. How to master the gondola ride:

Seek the middle of the cabin

The edges of the gondola swing significantly more than the center. Standing in the middle of the cabin noticeably spares your balance organ and gives you more stability.

Look at the mountain station

Looking directly straight down automatically triggers discomfort in many people. Instead, fix your eyes on the mountain station, a peak, or the horizon – this gives your eye a reliable and calm anchor point.

Find a firm hold

Be sure to use the handrails in the cabin. Through this so-called haptic contact, your brain immediately knows better about your body position in space and you stand much more securely and relaxed overall.

Traveling with children

Many parents know the feeling: Between the ages of 2 and 12, a child's balance system reacts particularly sensitively. However, many unpleasant moments can be gently avoided with the right planning.

The right child seat and a good view

For safety reasons, children must of course always be correctly secured in their child seat. As soon as their age and height allow it, however, a seat with a good forward view can enormously help to reduce motion sickness. A clear view makes it easier for the child's brain to properly process the movement of the journey.

Audiobooks instead of tablets

Movies and games on a tablet are unfortunately absolutely counterproductive when it comes to motion sickness, as the eyes remain rigidly fixed on the screen. Pack exciting audiobooks or their favorite music instead. This allows their eyes to wander out the window while their mind is wonderfully distracted.

Viativity for older children

Artificial Horizon

4-2-6 Paced Breathing

Anxiety, stress, and malaise often amplify each other. This gently guided breathing exercise (inhale for 4 seconds, hold for 2, exhale for 6) specifically activates your resting nerve (parasympathetic nervous system) and dampens physical stress reactions – perfect for rising flight anxiety or severe nervousness.

100 Hz Sound Modulation

Recent research shows that our inner ear also reacts to very specific vibrations. A deep 100 Hz tone, played unobtrusively through your headphones, gently stimulates the balance system and can help reduce susceptibility to discomfort.

P6 Acupressure

Gentle stimulation of the P6 point (Nei-Kuan) on the wrist sends calming impulses to your stomach. Viativity visually guides you to find this point yourself at the very first signs of discomfort and massage it gently – a proven accompaniment entirely without aids.

Mental Rotation

This tool is ideal for your preparation. Studies show: A trained spatial imagination enormously helps the brain to process conflicting movement data more efficiently. Practice this playfully on the couch a few weeks before your vacation.

Body Stability

Those who stand stably travel more relaxed. Body Stability is a pure audio and haptic exercise for your training at home. You train your balance and core stability with your eyes closed to give your body more resilience for the journey beforehand.

Focus Training

By concentrating strongly on a demanding visual task, you distract your brain and force it to simply blank out disturbing movement stimuli. This little tool is wonderful for gentle habituation in quieter phases of travel.
